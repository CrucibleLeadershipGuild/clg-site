Most self-evaluations are written the night before they are due, and most read like a list of tasks. That is a missed chance. Your manager is probably writing several reviews, often partly from memory, and your self-evaluation is the raw material they will work from. If you make it specific and easy to lift sentences from, you can shape how your year is described.

This guide shows a simple structure, three before-and-after rewrites, how to handle a bad stretch honestly, and a short template you can fill in today.

## Why most self-evaluations fail

Three patterns show up again and again:

- **The task list.** "Worked on the migration. Attended planning meetings. Supported the team." It says what you were busy with, not what changed because of you.
- **The shrug.** "I think I did okay this year" or "I hope I met expectations." Hedging makes your manager do the work of deciding whether you did.
- **The vague superlative.** "Hard worker, great team player, strong communicator." Anyone could write these, so they tell the reader nothing.

## The one rule: what you did, why it mattered, what happens next

Every strong sentence in a self-evaluation answers three questions:

1. **What did you do?** A specific action, project or decision, not a category of work.
2. **Why did it matter?** The result for a customer, a teammate, the schedule, quality or cost. Use a number when you honestly have one, and plain words when you do not.
3. **What happens next?** What you will do with it, or what you would like to take on, so the sentence points forward.

Compare "I helped with onboarding" with "I wrote a one-page onboarding checklist after two new hires struggled in their first week, and the next three hires needed far fewer questions. I would like to own onboarding for the whole team next year." The second version gives your manager a result they can repeat and a request they can act on.

## Build the raw material before you write

The best self-evaluations are not written from memory. Spend 15 to 30 minutes collecting evidence first:

- Your calendar for the year, to remember projects and meetings that shaped the work
- Sent email, chat threads and documents you wrote
- Tickets, pull requests, reports or deliverables you finished
- Thanks, praise or feedback you received, including critical feedback
- Goals from your last review, so you can report on each one directly

For next year, keep a running note and spend five minutes a week adding what you shipped, decided, fixed, taught or learned, with any number you can attach. In a year, the writing takes minutes.

## A structure that works

Unless your company gives you a form, use four sections. If it does give you a form, map these onto its questions.

1. **Results.** Your three to five most important outcomes, ranked by impact, not by date. Use the rule above for each one.
2. **How you worked.** How you collaborated, how you handled setbacks, who you helped. This is where you cover the work that does not show up in a ticket.
3. **Growth.** One or two things you got better at, and any feedback you acted on. Name a specific change you made.
4. **What is next.** What you want more of: scope, a skill, a project, a path. Add what support would help. Managers cannot plan around a goal you did not share.

Aim for one page unless the form asks for more. A tight page that a busy manager reads beats five pages they skim.

## Before and after: three rewrites

These examples are illustrative. Swap in your own facts and only claim numbers you can support.

**Rewrite 1: the vague superlative**

- **Before:** "I was a team player and helped out wherever needed."
- **After:** "When two teammates left in June, I took over onboarding for the three new hires and wrote a one-page checklist. All three were working independently within a month. I would like to keep owning onboarding and turn the checklist into a standard for the team."

**Rewrite 2: the task list**

- **Before:** "Worked on the customer portal migration."
- **After:** "I led the customer portal migration across four teams. We finished by the August deadline with no unplanned downtime, and I wrote a runbook so the next migration needs less coordination. Next, I would like to lead the billing migration using the same approach."

**Rewrite 3: the growth area that goes nowhere**

- **Before:** "I need to improve my communication."
- **After:** "My status updates were sometimes late this year, which meant my manager heard about slipped dates in meetings. Since September I send a short update every Friday. I would welcome feedback on whether it has the right level of detail."

Notice the third one. It names a real weakness, shows a fix already in motion, and asks for input. That reads as maturity, not weakness.

## How to cover a bad quarter or a missed goal

Do not hide it and do not bury it. Your manager almost certainly knows, and a self-evaluation that skips it looks unaware. A simple pattern works:

1. **State what happened in one plain sentence,** with no excuses and no blame.
2. **Say what you learned or changed.**
3. **Show what you have already done about it.**

For example: "I missed the Q3 launch date for the reporting feature because I underestimated the testing work. I now break estimates into build and verification steps and review them with the team before committing, and the Q4 release shipped on schedule."

If outside factors played a real part, such as a reprioritization or a staffing gap, mention them once, factually, and then move on to what you controlled. Long explanations sound defensive.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Writing it at the last minute.** It shows, and you will forget your best work from the early months.
- **Copying your job description.** Your manager knows your duties. They need your results.
- **Comparing yourself to coworkers.** Describe your own impact and let your manager do the comparing.
- **Leaving out numbers you could easily add.** Even approximate, honest figures ("about half", "three of four") beat none.
- **Giving a fake weakness.** "I work too hard" and "I am a perfectionist" are not growth areas, and managers recognize them.
- **Surprising your manager.** If a conflict or a missed goal matters, they should hear it from you before they read it.
- **Going on too long.** One strong page is better than three padded ones.

## A short template you can fill in today

Copy these prompts into your own document and finish each sentence.

- **Result 1:** "I [action], which [result for the team, customer or schedule]. Next, I plan to [follow-up or request]."
- **Result 2 and 3:** Repeat the same pattern.
- **How I worked:** "I helped [person or team] by [action], and when [setback happened] I [what you did]."
- **Growth:** "I improved at [skill] by [specific change]. I would like feedback on [one thing]."
- **A miss:** "I [what happened]. I learned [lesson], and I now [change you made]."
- **What is next:** "I would like more of [scope, skill or project]. It would help to have [support or time]."
